When 80 Doesn't
Mean the Same

A falling mark doesn't always mean a falling position.

My analytics program once predicted 80 on a mathematics quiz. I got 55—highest in my group. I started collecting old NDC report cards to find out how often a student's raw score and standing among others tell different stories.

01 / Where it started

A result I couldn't explain with my model.

While building the Bangladesh Academic Analytics System, I used past marks to estimate my next mathematics quiz result. The program predicted at least 80. I got 55. That was the highest mark in my group.

The score made me wonder what the program had missed. Exams don't always test the same things or get marked in quite the same way. What can we really conclude from a rise or fall in marks?

When a student's marks change, how often does their standing among others taking the same assessment move in another direction?
02 / Why start at NDC?

A report card holds more than one kind of score.

At Notre Dame College in Dhaka, students take monthly quizzes alongside larger internal examinations. Their Term, Sent-Up 1 and Sent-Up 2 reports bring those results together. These are college assessments, not national HSC board-exam results. The papers aren't identical, and the combined totals can reuse earlier marks.

That gives me a specific problem to investigate with real, repeated assessments. It's also why I need to separate the original exam marks from the weighted report totals before comparing anything.

03 / The research question

When do the two stories diverge?

A raw mark tells us what a student earned. Their relative standing tells us how that mark compares with other participating students on the same exam. Neither answers every question by itself.

Main question

For the same students taking comparable quizzes, how often do raw marks and relative standing change in opposite directions?

This will be measured, not assumed. The analysis may find frequent disagreement, little disagreement or patterns that vary by subject.

Does the answer depend on the method?

I'll check whether the picture changes when relative standing is measured with percentiles rather than standardized scores, and when incomplete records are excluded.

What should my software do with that information?

If the two measures sometimes tell different stories, I want to consider how a dashboard could show that uncertainty rather than quietly choosing a single judgment. Testing a redesigned interface would be a later step.

04 / Planned method

Follow the same students across exams.

I'm collecting Term, Sent-Up 1 and Sent-Up 2 report cards from the NDC HSC 2026 cohort. They include individual quiz marks and major-exam results. The report cards' weighted totals will be treated separately from the component exams.

01

Read the records

Check the reports and record subjects, papers, quizzes, examination scores and missing values without guessing.

02

Keep the group consistent

Compare quizzes within the same subject using students whose marks are available for both assessments.

03

Check the two trends

Look for cases where raw marks and relative standing change in opposite directions, and see whether the pattern holds under other measures.

Important limit: relative standing in a volunteer sample isn't an official NDC rank or a direct measure of exam difficulty. The aim is to test descriptions, not prove a student's true ability.
05 / Who might care?

These numbers are read by people.

In a college setting, marks can shape how students describe themselves and how others talk about their progress. I'm not claiming my study will change any decisions. But it could help make the questions behind those decisions clearer.

Students

If a mark drops, does that always reflect a similar drop in standing? Seeing both may help a student ask a better question about what happened.

Teachers

Could looking at the distribution of marks from a shared assessment provide context when discussing progress? It would still need to be interpreted with classroom knowledge.

Families

A report card can be easy to reduce to one number. This research asks what that number leaves out, without asking anyone to ignore the mark itself.

People building education software

My own program produced a prediction I couldn't defend. That raises a design question: what should an analytics system display, and where should it admit uncertainty?

These are potential uses to explore, not proven benefits or claims about how NDC currently evaluates students.

06 / Why the answer matters

Either outcome teaches me something.

I don't want to build the research around a conclusion I've already decided is true.

If disagreements appear often

Then a single raw-score trend might leave out useful context in this sample. It would be worth testing whether showing both measures makes an academic dashboard more informative.

If disagreements are rare

That matters too. It would suggest that the extra measure often adds little for the quizzes studied, or that any disagreement depends on specific subjects and assessments.

The point isn't to replace marks with ranking. It's to find out when one number leaves out part of the story.

A boundary I don't want to cross: comparison with a volunteer sample isn't the same as an official rank, and a lower sample average doesn't prove an exam was harder. A student's standing can change because the rest of the group changes. This study cannot explain individual circumstances or rank students and schools across Bangladesh.

Sometimes the two trends really do disagree.

I ran a first comparison using 20 students' report-card-backed histories. For each pair of consecutive quizzes, I compared raw marks with standing among the same students who had marks for both quizzes.

In one Mathematics example, a student's mark went from 44 to 40, but their standing in the matched group improved from the 29th to the 65th percentile. The raw score alone missed what happened around it.

With a minimum change of five marks and ten percentile points, I still found 79 disagreements in 628 comparisons (12.6%). That gives me something to explore further; it doesn't establish which paper was harder or which measure is fairer.

The limit: this is a small, self-selected group. Several comparisons come from the same students, so they aren't independent observations. Sample standing isn't an official NDC rank. Generated software-test records were excluded.
